India: A Bird’s-Eye View (Whyte, 1943)

A short wartime Chatham House pamphlet by Sir Frederick Whyte, K.C.S.I., giving a five-chapter “bird’s-eye” of historic India, the British Raj, the political crisis of 1942 (Cripps Mission, Pakistan, Congress “Quit India”), the Indian States under paramountcy, and India’s part in the Second World War, closed by a table of parallel dates and statistical tables.

Summary

Printed in the United States for the Royal Institute of International Affairs (RIIA disclaimer: the Institute expresses no opinion; opinions are the author’s). Title-page date 1943; Rutgers copy stamped 12-15-43. Companion in this wiki to the larger 1941 Chatham House symposium Modern India and the West (O’Malley): Whyte is the short contemporary briefing while O’Malley is the specialist survey.

Whyte opens by placing India apart even from the rest of Asia: a sub-continent of Himalaya, Indo-Gangetic plain, and Deccan; monsoon and irrigation; 1941 census “not far short of 400 millions”; a land of peasants near the margin of subsistence; Hinduism as the social frame that left politics a modern Western growth. The Raj chapter runs from Elizabeth’s East India Company charter (31 December 1600), paired with Charles I’s Massachusetts company, through Bombay (1661/1669), Company-to-Crown 1858, Queen’s Proclamation, English education and Congress, Tilak and Gandhi, 1917 Declaration, 1919 Act and Statutory Commission, Round Table Conference and federal hope, to the 1935 Act and the outbreak of war with India automatically at war when the King was at war.

Chapter III treats the Cripps Mission of early 1942 as the outstanding political event of that spring: not a barren failure, Whyte argues, but a new climate and an accelerated movement along the line of 1917. Pakistan (the “istan” coinage), League resolutions, Atlantic Charter Point 3, the Draft Declaration (union, secession analogue, treaty), Sikh and Liberal responses, Rajagopalachari, and Congress rejection (1 May) through the Bombay “Quit India” resolution (August 1942) occupy the narrative. Chapter IV sizes the States (712,000 sq. miles; 92 million people; 39 per cent of area, 23 per cent of population; 21-gun salute group) and the doctrine of paramountcy after the Mutiny. Chapter V turns to the Indian Army, industry, and the wartime reduction of India’s sterling debt. Appendices: parallel dates from Vasco da Gama / Akbar / Vijayanagar 1565 / Company 1600 onward; statistical tables of area, population, cities, religions, languages, communities, occupation, factories, metals, railways, cotton mills, and trade 1937–40; maps of political divisions and communications (present in the scan as listed, not as recovered images in this extract).

Contents

Printed contents: I Historic India; II The British Raj; III The Crisis of 1942; IV The Indian States; V India in the War; Appendix I Table of Parallel Dates; Appendix II Statistical Tables; Maps (India: Political Divisions; India: Communications).

I — Historic India

“There is no country in the world which presents a picture of greater variety than India.” Contrast with the West; India as an element the European must unclothe his mind to enter; Maya and reversed values. Three regions; monsoon (Upper Sind 3.6 inches vs Cherrapunji 400); irrigation and railways said almost to have driven famine out. 1941 census approaching 400 million; density from Cochin 4,000 per sq. mile to Baluchistan 6.5; two-thirds of the people on one-quarter of the land. Peasants; James VI’s “beggar’s mantle fringed with gold”; Gandhi and the charkha; Hinduism, illiteracy, moneylender debt, and the cow as obstacles to agricultural reform. Literacy 9.3 per cent Bengal to 4.2 per cent NWFP. About 70 per cent Hindu, 22 per cent Mohammedan, plus Christians, Sikhs, Jains, Parsees, Jews. Cohesion by Hinduism (belief, custom, law, ritual) rather than monarchy or secular law; political life a modern Western growth. Islam as successive conquest culminating in the Mogul Empire; a more radical distinction from Hinduism than Catholic/Protestant in Europe.

II — The British Raj

Opens 31 December 1600, Elizabeth’s East India Company charter; 1628 Charles I’s Massachusetts company as the twin seed of empire East and West, now converging in the twentieth century (Statute of Westminster, Cripps). Bombay from Catherine of Braganza (1661) transferred to the Company (1669); charters Cromwell 1657, Charles II 1661, Anne 1709; Company as “Sovereign Corporation.” Reform Act 1832 Parliament and James Mill’s caution (“engraft on despotism the natural fruits of liberty”). Transfer to the Crown; John Company’s last instructions on the civil and military services. Drain/interest argument answered by exchange of British manufactures and Indian produce. Social reform against infanticide, human sacrifice, child marriage, temple prostitution, widow remarriage ban. Queen’s Proclamation: good government, justice, toleration, progress, and Indian employment — no word of democracy or responsibility except to Crown and Imperial Parliament. Tilak (force) and Gandhi (opposite tactics) as two campaigns from Hindu revival; English education as the vehicle of political awakening; National Congress. First World War Indian troops and infant industry; 1917 Declaration; 1919 Act and ten-year Statutory Commission; Round Table Conference and proposed Federal Union of British India and the States; Irwin/Halifax 1931; war outbreak with Congress refusing co-operation because India was automatically at war.

III — The Crisis of 1942

“The Cripps Mission to India was the outstanding political event of the early spring of 1942.” Whyte refuses “barren failure”: new climate; Declaration as heir of 1917, not a radical departure. Pakistan etymology (Punjab, Afghan Frontier, Kashmir + istan); League’s reaffirmation of Pakistan and disapproval of a constitution based on India’s economic and political unity. Atlantic Charter Point 3 (“right of all peoples to choose the form of Government”). Draft Declaration: new constitution, possible separate union of same full status, treaty with H.M. Government. Sikhs: “resist by all possible means the separation of the Punjab from the All-India Union.” Sapru and Jayakar on the Defence portfolio. All-India Congress Committee rejects British proposals 1 May; Rajagopalachari’s Madras friends urge acknowledging the League claim and talking to Jinnah. Working Committee to All-India Committee in Bombay (7 August): “Quit India”; Labour, Depressed Classes, M. N. Roy’s Left. Whyte: India as a whole in the war; Congress had failed to gauge prevailing sentiment; Sir Ramaswami Mudaliar on Pacific War Council and War Cabinet.

IV — The Indian States

Five 21-gun States named: Baroda, Gwalior, Hyderabad, Kashmir, Mysore. 712,000 square miles vs British India’s one million; 92 million people vs 296 million (39 per cent of area, 23 per cent of population). Paramountcy: disputes settled by British officers’ rules or orders, not agreements; Patiala–Nabha arbitration by the Viceroy. Pre-Mutiny isolation of States; Mutiny as change of mind after Company unrest. Post-1858 suzerainty “eagerly acknowledged by the Chiefs”; Hyderabad sanad 1862, succession invalid unless recognized by the King-Emperor. Ideal of provinces and states working out destinies and combining for imperial purposes.

V — India in the War

Martial spirit of the Army in India (British and Indian) as the Raj’s claim to have enlisted communities in a common purpose. Economic shift: commerce before industry; heavy industry in the wake of railways; pre-1914 iron and steel. Wartime finance: India’s sterling obligations reduced (figure given for 1941–42, £90,000,000, “will soon be reduced to zero”); British investment in railways, irrigation, and public works becoming Indian-owned free of interest encumbrance.

Appendix I — Table of Parallel Dates

Facing columns of general / Indian dates. Sample openings in the extract: 1497–8 voyage; 1556–1605 Akbar; 1565 fall of Vijayanagar; 1600 East India Company; 1602 Dutch EIC; 1604 French EIC; 1634 English trade throughout Mogul dominions; 1639 Madras / Fort St George.
