Summary

Mill finishes the Hindu survey with arts (architecture, weaving, jewellery; Chidambaram and Srirangam as European marvels he discounts), literature (almost all verse; history as narrative poems without resemblance to fact), and a long “General Reflections” on where Hindus sit on the scale of civilization — written, he says, because British government must be adapted to that scale, and because Britain has mistaken a people who have made “but a few of the earliest steps” for a highly civilized one. Wilson’s note at the head of those reflections: government should see Hindus as they are now, with a bias in their favour.

Book III: India at the Cape opening is a Muslim government and force over a Hindu population. Northern invasions (Scythians, Darius’s Indian satrapy, Alexander), then Ghaznavids to their expulsion; first Ghurid dynasty; second Ghurid/Afghan line to the Mughals; Mughal dynasty from the break-up of Timur’s empire and Baber, 1525, to its close; last a comparison of Muslim and Hindu civilization. Wilson’s closer on the comparison: Muslim literature never enlightened the people; governments military despotism; religion nearer truth than Hindu idolatry but as full of trifling observance and less humane in its intolerance than Hindu polytheism.

Book II (continued) — Of the Hindus

Chapter VIII — The Arts

Arts, necessary or ornamental, as a mark of progress. Hindus, Mill says, little courted the pleasures of the arts: houses of the great mean and almost unfurnished (Sir Thomas Roe: mud, one storey, except Surat stone; great men in tents for want of inheritance); food simple; dress distinguished only by fineness of texture. First arts of a rising people: hunting, shelter, covering; hence weaving and architecture early, then jewellery from the savage love of ornament. These three — architecture, weaving, jewellery — are the only arts for which the Hindus have been celebrated; except weaving they remained low. Robertson on the Chidambaram (Chillambrum) gopuram (122 feet, copper plates, 1332 × 936 feet) and Orme on Srirangam’s seven enclosures are treated as size plus “loose eulogy,” not proof of high civilization. Inferior as manufacturers to the Chinese; Jones’s slighting of Chinese arts is turned against his Hindu praise. Wilson: Hindu iron-smelting, welding, and steel are ancient (Ctesias); English iron-casting is recent.

Chapter IX — Literature

Literature as the surest inference to civilization, because it is the friend of useful knowledge. First literature is poetry (passions before speculation; verse as memory before writing). Hindu literature, Mill says, has always remained at this first stage: almost everything in verse; for history only narrative poems that “depart from all resemblance to truth and nature”; laws and sacred and scientific books in verse, as among other rude nations. Wilson: “permanent signs” cannot mean writing, which Hindus have had as long as a literature; metre continues because Sanskrit metre is easier, not because of German carmina antiqua.

Chapter X — General Reflections

To place Hindus on the scale of civilization is curiosity and, for Britain charged with their government, “the highest practical importance”: no scheme of government works unless fitted to the people’s state. If Britain has taken Hindus for a highly civilized people when they have made only the earliest steps, measures of government will miss the mark. The preceding induction — religion, laws, government, manners, arts, sciences, literature — is offered as the material for a correct judgment. Mill then attacks received claims of enormous riches found by the first Muslim conquerors (Ferishta followed by Dow as Eastern exaggeration that refutes itself); from his induction, poverty and wretchedness of the great body of the people in earlier as in European-witnessed times. Wilson’s head-note: British measures have little to do with Hindus in the days of Manu; see them as they are, with a bias in their favour; “We shall not promote their advance in civilization by treating them as little better than barbarians.”

Book III — The Mohammedans

Chapter I — First northern invasions to the expulsion of the Ghaznavid dynasty

When Europe opened the Cape route, the people of Book II had long been under a foreign race: Muslim government and force over a Hindu population, new forms in some departments of state, military command in foreign hands. Hindustan always subject to north-west incursions. Justin’s Scythians; Darius Hystaspes’ Indian satrapy (Herodotus: the most valuable of twenty; Rennell’s Indus/Punjab/Kabul/Kandahar — Wilson: Mill misquotes Rennell on Delhi). Alexander did not go as far as Darius. Then the Ghaznavid line in India to its expulsion, closing on Khosroo II, Mohammed of Ghur taking Ghazni/Kandahar, Multan, and advancing to Lahore.

Chapter II — First Ghurid dynasty to the second Ghurid or Afghan dynasty

After Khosroo’s defeat and death, Mohammed leaves India to a viceroy, returns to Ghazni, five years later marches on Ajmer, takes Tiberhind, is defeated and wounded near Thanesar by the rajas of Ajmer and Delhi, then returns with Turks, Persians, and Afghans. The combined rajas (Mill repeats the inflated 150 kings, 300,000 horse, 3,000 elephants) send an insulting offer of unmolested retreat; Mohammed feigns humility, crosses the river by night, and fights in four lines with a chosen horse reserve. The chapter is the first Ghurid hold in India until the second, Afghan, line.

Chapter III — Second Ghurid or Afghan dynasty to the commencement of the Mughal dynasty

Afghan/Delhi-sultanate narrative to the eve of Baber. The extract’s closer of this span: Ibrahim of Delhi twenty years against rebellion; in 1525 Baber, descendant of Timur, deprives him of throne and life.

Chapter IV — From the commencement to the close of the Mughal dynasty

Opens on the death of Shahrokh and the dissolution of Timur’s empire; Turks under the successors press Mesopotamia; Ismael the adventurer (1500). Then Baber’s line through the Mughal emperors to the dynasty’s close (printed contents from p. 319). This is Mill’s continuous political narrative of the Mughals, not a Company chapter.

Chapter V — Comparison of civilization: Muslim conquerors and Hindus

After the transactions of Book III, Mill asks the stage of civilization of the invading nations, and whether Hindu civilization rose or fell under Muslim ascendency. Western Asia (Persians, Arabians, even Turks) a degree higher than nations further east. The actual conquerors of Hindustan he places on a level with Arabians and Persians at their height: eastern Persian empire, Bactria and Transoxiana; language of Balkh as the elegant Persian, the tongue God uses mildly to the cherubim. Wilson’s end-note: Muslim literature original in part but less agreeable than Hindu except history; science borrowed from Greeks and Hindus; magnificence of the caliphs brief; letters confined to court and camp; governments military despotism; religion nearer truth than Hindu idolatry, in practice as full of trifling observance, and in intolerance less humane than Hindu polytheism.
