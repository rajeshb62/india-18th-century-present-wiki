A Short History of the East India Company (Russell, 1793)

A second-edition London pamphlet, attributed to Francis Russell, written as Parliament prepared to renew the East India Company’s exclusive trade (term ending 1794): origin of the old and new companies, India-House 3 per cent annuities, purchased versus conquered forts, expenses of conquest, chartered rights, the “monopoly” charge, foreign rivals, trade and shipping, profits, the Company as sovereign and merchant, objections to innovation, a regulated opening for private merchants, then a postscript, a law discussion of Masulipatam and the Northern Circars, four-year sales figures, and an abridgment of the 1793 Act.

Summary

Title-page (OCR): A Short History of the East India Company: exhibiting a state of their affairs, abroad and at home, political and commercial; the nature and magnitude of their commerce, and its relative connection with the government and revenues of India; and a discussion on the question of right to the conquered territories in India; also remarks on the danger and impolicy of innovation, and the practical means of ensuring all the good effects of a free trade to the manufacturers of Great Britain and Ireland, by matter of regulation, without disturbing the established system. The second edition, with some additions. To which is added, an abridgment of the new act. London: John Sewell, Cornhill, and John Debrett, Piccadilly, 1793. NYPL copy (Astor, Lenox and Tilden Foundations, 1910), marked Russell, Copy 2. Wrapper/OCLC 40214405 names Francis Russell.

The introduction presents the tract as information for senators on the eve of a “most serious investigation” of the India and China trade. The second edition reprints a well-received first; a new section answers official doubt of the editor’s claim (ch. III) that the Company has exclusive property and revenue of the five Northern Circars.

The argument throughout is that exclusive joint-stock trade rests on parliamentary compact and purchase, not mere prerogative; that territorial revenues have not yet reimbursed conquest debts; that “chartered monopoly” is a popular odium; that a fully open trade would endanger the political chain with India; and that private merchants can be given the benefits of the trade by regulation (especially cheaper freight) without breaking the system. Postscript (dated as in the former edition, February 1793): the Company’s published statement of probable net surplus of territorial revenues and trade profits in profound peace. Additional ch. XV: Crown versus Company at law over Masulipatam and the Northern Circars, as the 1781 compact nears expiry and ministers offer propositions for continuing government and a new partition of surplus. Closing: four years of India/China sales, duties, exports, drawbacks; abridgment of the Act settling government and trade and appropriating territorial revenues and profits of trade (Charter Act 1793).

Contents

Printed contents: Introduction; I Origin of the Old and New Companies, with their Union; II Origin of the 3 per cent. annuities transferrable at the India House; III Forts, factories, and territories (purchase vs conquest); IV Expenses of the Indian conquests; V Permanent and temporary rights; VI How far “chartered monopoly” applies; VII Former plans for varying the trade; VIII Present state of foreign countries’ trade with India and China; IX Returns of trade before and after territorial acquisition; debts and claims on the public; X Nature and extent of trade and shipping; XI Profits of trade; XII Company as sovereigns and merchants; XIII Recapitulation, parliamentary objections, innovation; XIV Practical means for private merchants without disturbing the system; Postscript; Additional ch. XV (territories; Crown vs Company); Account of imports sold in the last four years; Abridgment of the new Act.

Introduction

Liberty of the press as the means by which practical knowledge reaches Parliament. The editor’s “particular situation and line of life” as his warrant for setting out the India and China trade. Object: a connected brief of origin and progress of the trade and companies before the present state. Second edition: added section on the Northern Circars after the third chapter’s opinion was questioned in high official situations.

Chapter I — Origin of the Old and New Companies, with their Union

Sea passage to India not discovered until the later fifteenth century; first successful English incorporation The London East India Company, charter 31 December 1600. Factories in India and Persia; Crown charters without parliamentary sanction until after the Restoration. Interlopers; 1693 default on a duty voiding charters; revival on three years’ notice; 1698 Act borrowing two millions at 8 per cent. and incorporating a General Society and The English East India Company; Old Company buys into the loan; union 1702, confirmed under Godolphin 1708. Warehouses, shipping, settlements (including Bombay and St Helena) vest in the United Company. General Society’s separate traders shrink to £7,000 then absorbed. Footnotes list forts (Mazagon, Mahim, Fort St George, Fort William, Bombay, St Helena, &c.) and factories (Surat, Agra, Cossimbuzar, Dacca, Hugli, Patna, &c.).

Chapter II — 3 per cent. annuities at the India House

Public debt of £4,200,000 to the Company; interest reduced in 1750 to 3 per cent., £126,000 a year. Salt and stamp duties as securities. Premium of £200,000 and interest cuts for extensions of exclusive trade; 1744 loan of one million at 3 per cent. (raised on bonds, not added to dividend capital of £3,200,000). Last renewal 1781 (21 Geo. III c. 65): exclusive trade to 1794 for £400,000 in discharge of public claims before 1 March 1781; after 8 per cent. dividend, surplus of net trade and revenues three-fourths to the Public, one-fourth to the Company — no such surplus yet because of Indian war debts. Compulsory tea stock (1784) and new subscriptions to five millions (1786, 1789).

Chapter III — Forts, factories, and territories (purchase vs conquest)

Before suggesting future trade regulations, distinguish possessions held in perpetuity from those claimed by the public. Purchase versus conquest; Clive-era military acquisitions; Commons resolution 1773 that acquisitions under military force or treaty belong to the State; earlier royal licence to the Company to cede conquests except European settlements.

Chapter IV — Expenses of the Indian conquests

Answer to the claim that territorial revenues and commercial funds are so blended that conquest cost cannot be known, and receipts may be set against payments forever. Russell: if revenues stayed with the Company until debts were cleared, that close might be acceptable; as things stand, land revenues have fallen short of reimbursing territorial disbursements by the full amount of debts abroad and at home (current commercial debts excepted). Method: an account at home, since remittances come only through investments.

Chapter V — Permanent and temporary rights

Against the “prevailing error” that corporate capacity, joint-stock trade, and chartered rights all determine in 1794 unless renewed. Distinguishes rights that need renewal from those that stand in the way of laying the trade open.

Chapter VI — “Chartered monopoly”

The phrase is meant to excite popular odium. Old Company held exclusive trade by voluntary Crown grant on prerogative; the present Company holds by purchase from the Public on parliamentary compact. King William’s charter of incorporation is distinguished from those purchased rights.

Chapter VII — Former plans for varying the trade

No grave proposal to throw the trade wholly open to chance. Serious alternatives were a Regulated Open Company (every merchant trades on his own stock under company regulation): the 1600 company and the General Society both grew into joint-stock companies. Interlopers, Mogul resentment, five years’ interruption, a king’s ship and proclamation to force interlopers under Company jurisdiction.

Chapter VIII — Foreign countries’ trade with India and China

Portuguese, Spaniards, French, Danes, Dutch: privileges of free trade and factories. French, Danes, Dutch mostly by companies like England’s; Portuguese by houses and individuals; Spanish trade dwindled; Swedish company chiefly China, no continental settlements. Commutation Act 1784 and lower tea duties: Company undersells smugglers via Ostend, Dunkirk; Swedish, Danish, Embden companies almost annihilated; Trieste Company stopped. Dutch spice monopoly (pepper excepted); distress after the American-war rupture with England; loans from the States General. French failures; present French company buys from the English Company. Private adventurers from Sweden, Denmark, Ostend, France, Portugal, America as a caution against small-capital Asiatic speculation.

Chapter IX — Trade returns before and after territory; debts; indemnification

Before 1757, sale amount of imports rarely exceeded two millions in a year. Investments from exports, bullion, and servants’ bills on London. Acquisition of territories changes exports and imports; present debts; claims on the public for expenses of acquiring and preserving conquered provinces.

Chapter X — Nature and extent of trade and shipping

Exports: House of Commons has printed three Directors’ reports to the Board of Commerce, reprinted by the Company; Russell recites them rather than recompute species, quantities, and values. Shipping employed in the India and China trade.

Chapter XI — Profits of trade

Published accounts do not separate profit and loss on export trade: merchandise blended with military stores; merchandise charges blended with recruits, gratuities, pensions, law. About 1780, export trade and duty on private trade cleared a medium profit equal to £2 12s. 6d. per cent. a year on the old capital of £3,200,000; China export profits computed at £65,000 a year; India exports doubted to yield real profit. Import sales 1791–93: produce exceeds invoice plus customs, freight, and charges.

Chapter XII — Sovereigns and merchants

Compact of 1781: after 8 per cent. dividend, surplus three-fourths public, one-fourth Company. War and increased civil and military establishments 1779–82 made those funds deficient. In 1783 the Public agreed to forgo participation until certain debts were discharged.

Chapter XIII — Recapitulation; parliamentary objections; innovation

Company threatened with serious opposition to exclusive trade. Members of the legislature and the public need the Company’s acknowledged rights and how they are upheld. Circumspection before any change of system that might lose or divert this branch of British commerce (employment, revenues, re-export carrying trade, Eastern articles at reasonable prices).

Chapter XIV — Practical means without disturbing the system

Freight: advancement of export and import depends on conveyance at a reasonable rate, long fruitlessly attempted, so manufacturers and merchants will adventure on their own risk. Directors will find it hard to persuade of present freight rates. Object: private merchant and public get the ultimate benefits inside exclusive limits without endangering the political connection with India.

Postscript (February 1793, as in the former edition)

Since the sheets went to press, the Company has published a statement of probable net annual surplus of territorial revenues in India and profits on trade in profound peace — not a medium of former actual receipts and disbursements.

Additional Chapter XV — Masulipatam and the Northern Circars

Question as between the Crown and the Company of the town, port, and district of Masulipatam and of the Northern Circars on the Coromandel coast. 1781 compact near expiry; ministers offer propositions to continue the system of government and a new partition of surplus.

Account of imports (last four years)

India and China goods sold at the Company’s sales; duties and customs; amount exported each year; drawbacks on exportation.

Abridgment of the new Act

Act for settling the government and trade of India, and for the appropriation of the territorial revenues and profits of trade (the 1793 charter legislation).
