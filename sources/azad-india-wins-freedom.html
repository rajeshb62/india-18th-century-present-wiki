India Wins Freedom (Azad, complete version)

Maulana Abul Kalam Azad (1888–1958; born Muhiyuddin Ahmad / Firoz Bakht). Autobiographical narrative of the decade of transfer of power, dictated in conversation to Humayun Kabir and approved by Azad sentence by sentence. Dedicated to Jawaharlal Nehru, “friend and comrade.” Orient Blackswan complete version (court-released 1988 text; this EPUB Orient Blackswan 2018 / e-edition lineage from 2011). Local file: Anna’s Archive EPUB hash 44bc93b0db4856d43d1b4d2b783e8424.

Completeness

Complete as the published complete-version single volume.

  • Front matter: About the author; title (the complete version); copyright; dedication; Publishers’ Note (Oct. 1988); Contents; Preface to the 1959 edition (Kabir, 15 Mar. 1958); Prospectus (planned first-volume synopsis to 1937).
  • Body: sixteen chapters (Congress in Office through Divided India) + Epilogue.
  • Appendixes 1–5: Kabir’s editor’s note on sealed passages; Cripps Draft Declaration (29 Mar. 1942); correspondence with Cripps; further documents through the Mountbatten / 3 June plan materials (Punjab and Bengal Muslim-majority districts by 1941 census).
  • Restored passages marked with asterisks at beginning and end, per Publishers’ Note. Closer: end of Appendix 5 district lists. Planned Vol. I (to 1937) and Vol. III (after 1948) were never written.

Summary

Azad’s reading of how India won freedom and lost unity. Kabir spent evenings with him from about 1956; drafted in English; Azad revised repeatedly and, for the 1959 book, withheld roughly thirty pages of personal judgements under seal until thirty years after his death. The 1988 complete edition, ordered released by the Delhi High Court from the National Library and National Archives deposits, restores those passages (and Kabir’s earlier softennings) throughout the text. The narrative runs from the 1937 provincial elections and Congress office, through wartime Congress presidency, the Cripps Mission, Quit India and Ahmednagar Fort jail, the Simla Conference, the 1945–46 elections, the Cabinet Mission Plan (which Azad saw as the federal solution that could have held India together), the Interim Government, Mountbatten’s arrival, and partition. Azad’s through-line: partition was avoidable; Patel fell first for Mountbatten’s scheme; Nehru’s succession as Congress president after Azad was “perhaps the greatest blunder” of Azad’s political life; geography made a consolidated Muslim state impossible; Pakistan solved no communal problem and weakened Muslims on both sides of the new border.

Contents

About the author / front matter

Sketch of Azad: Mecca birth 1888; Al Hilal (Calcutta, 1912); opposition to Aligarh aloofness from Congress; Khilafat and Congress presidency; works including Tarjuman-ul-Quran. Kabir’s academic and political résumé as amanuensis. Dedication to Nehru.

Publishers’ Note (October 1988)

Manuscript handed over September 1958 after Azad’s death; fuller sealed text promised for 22 February 1988 (thirtieth death anniversary). Hand-over delayed by litigation; Justice B. N. Kirpal (Delhi High Court, 29 Sept. 1988) ordered identical National Library / National Archives copies released to Orient Longman for reproduction without alteration. “Additional matter” not a discrete thirty pages but scattered omissions and Kabir editorial softenings throughout. Asterisks mark major restorations. Prospectus and original appendixes retained.

Preface to the 1959 Edition (Humayun Kabir, 15 Mar. 1958)

Azad reluctant (health; dislike of personal talk); persuaded that a principal actor in the transfer of power owed a record to posterity. Method: evening conversations; English drafts; joint revision; completed draft September 1957; ~thirty pages of personal incidents sealed for National Library and National Archives; abridged draft November 1957; final approval Republic Day 1958. Book meant for November 1958 (seventieth birthday); Azad died first. Prospectus retained as synopsis of the never-written first volume (to 1937); a third volume after 1948 also intended. Royalties partly to Indian Council for Cultural Relations for essay prizes on Islam/Hinduism by citizens of India or Pakistan under thirty. Kabir records disagreements with some judgements but refuses to colour the narrative.

Prospectus (of the planned first volume)

Family from Herat in Babar’s days; Agra then Delhi; scholarly Mughal service (Jamaluddin under Akbar; Munawaruddin as Rukn-ul Mudarassin). Father Khairuddin to Mecca before/around 1857; Azad born Mecca 1888; family to Calcutta 1890. Traditional Arabic/Persian education; crisis of faith after reading Sir Syed; Curzon and the Partition of Bengal; contact with Shyam Sunder Chakravarty and Hindu-middle-class revolutionary groups then actively anti-Muslim. Stops as the gate into the 1937–48 narrative that fills this volume.

1 — Congress in Office

1937 elections under provincial autonomy: Congress absolute majority in five major provinces. Early reluctance to contest or take office under the 1935 Act; interim non-Congress ministries; then office as a trial of the organisation. Restored: Nariman denied Bombay leadership to satisfy Patel’s communal preferences; Parsee enquiry whitewash; Syed Mahmud passed over for Bihar chief ministership despite being the province’s top Congressman and AICC General Secretary. Congress ideals not lived up to. Glance back to C. R. Das’s practical federal foresight and the 1921 Prince of Wales boycott.

2 — War in Europe

Munich and Congress dissociation from British foreign policy that aided Fascist powers. Gandhiji’s deepening gloom and wish that India declare non-participation; Azad differs — Europe’s war is also India’s stake once Fascism threatens. Provincial Congress ministries still in office against this sombre backdrop.

3 — I Become Congress President

War breaks 3 September 1939; Poland falls. Azad elected Congress president unopposed in substance. Congress resolution on the war: no free India’s war without Indian consent; British policy in India unchanged. Expectancy and fear in India; presidential burden in a transformed international scene.

4 — A Chinese Interlude

Roosevelt’s concern for India’s voluntary war participation. Nehru’s pre-war visit to Chiang Kai-shek; Chiang and Madame arrive Delhi 9 February 1942; talks with Congress leaders. Bridge chapter into the spring of the Cripps Mission.

5 — The Cripps Mission

Deepening war crisis; expectation of a British shift. Stafford Cripps’s aide-mémoire and defence formula; Azad will not commit on a hypothetical. Draft Declaration (Dominion union; provincial non-accession / secession analogue; wartime defence reserved to H.M.G.). Congress concentration on present defence responsibility vs vague “National Government” that remains the Viceroy’s Executive Council. Failure of the Mission.

6 — Uneasy Interval

Disappointment and anger after Cripps. Azad reports to AICC: British attitude unhelpful since war’s outbreak; Congress had sought effective Indian responsibility for defence. Climate that feeds the Quit India resolution.

7 — Quit India

Working Committee resolution creates an electric atmosphere; wait for Government reaction; foreign press at Wardha. After passage, Mahadev Desai and Mira Ben. Arrests follow; the organisation goes into the wilderness of 1942–45.

8 — Ahmednagar Fort Jail

Nine Working Committee members with Azad: Nehru, Patel, Asaf Ali, etc. Military-barrack detention; iron platters; place of detention kept secret (futile). Jail routines, reading, internal debates; the long pause while the war turns.

9 — The Simla Conference

American pressure for an Indian settlement after 1942 arrests; wartime improvement in Europe renews it. Wavell’s Simla Conference: attempt at a reconstituted Executive Council; League–Congress deadlock over parity and League claim to all Muslim seats. Failure that sets up the post-war elections.

10 — General Elections

Doctors send Azad to Kashmir (Gulmarg). Viceroy announces general elections for the coming winter. Atom bombs on Japan — Azad still convinced there was no justification. Electoral contest that strengthens both Congress and League mandates for the Cabinet Mission round.

11 — The British Cabinet Mission

February 1946: a transformed India. Pethick-Lawrence’s announcement (17 Feb.); Attlee’s 15 March Commons speech (faults on both sides; no precedent in Indo-British relations). Mission arrives 23 March. Azad’s core constitutional conclusion: India must be federal; defence, communications, and foreign affairs only workable on an all-India basis. The Cabinet Mission Plan as the frame that could reconcile League fears with Indian unity — the solution he never abandons.

12 — Prelude to Partition

Political and communal problems seemed solved; Congress needs a new president. Restored: Azad’s decision not to continue, and his support of Nehru over Patel, as “perhaps the greatest blunder of my political life” — convinced that Patel as president would have held the Cabinet Mission Plan. AICC approval of the Plan; Nehru takes the chair; subsequent glosses and League withdrawal begin the unravelling.

13 — Interim Government

Parliamentary Committee forms the Interim Government. Azad presses for a Parsee seat (as at Simla); economist as first Indian Finance Member. League rage at being left out, then entry; Finance in League hands becomes a lever that later paralyses the Centre. Path into the Mountbatten months.

14 — The Mountbatten Mission

Mountbatten sworn 24 March 1947; tells Azad at once that Britain will transfer power. Communal deterioration (Calcutta, Noakhali, Bihar, Bombay, Punjab); European services losing heart; Congress–League deadlock inside the Executive Council. Central claim: Patel first fell for Mountbatten’s partition idea; Mountbatten then worked on Nehru; Pakistan had been for Jinnah in part a bargaining counter. Assam pressure despite non-Muslim majority. Administration lax as passions mount.

15 — The End of a Dream

Lingering hope Labour would not discard the Cabinet Mission Plan. Mountbatten’s plan published; first open discard of the Mission Plan for partition. Commonwealth question; Churchill’s hostility to the Mission Plan. Working Committee 3 June; Frontier / Khudai Khidmatgars; Gandhiji’s surprising conversion to the Mountbatten Plan; Khan brothers, Pakhtoonistan, and the Frontier referendum. Dream of united India ends.

16 — Divided India

14–15 August 1947: Mountbatten inaugurates Pakistan; India free at midnight — and wakes to massacre. Ten-year retrospect: Congress leaders had not accepted partition with conviction; Patel the greatest Congress supporter of it, yet even he did not think it best. 14 August as mourning for Hindus and Sikhs; Muslim League leaders left in India claim Jinnah deceived them — they had not grasped Pakistan’s implications. Hostage-and-reprisal doctrine applied in Delhi. Freedom with tragedy.

Epilogue

Gandhi’s assassination; failure of Delhi police/CID after the prior bomb; restored passage on Patel’s indifference during Gandhi’s fast for Muslim security and its effect on local officials. Jayaprakash Narayan and P. C. Ghosh hold the Home Minister responsible; Patel rallies the Congress Parliamentary Party. Sweets distributed in Gwalior and Jaipur; brief isolation of Hindu Mahasabha / RSS; Godse trial; Patel’s later heart attack. Freedom gained, unity lost. Pakistan’s leadership drawn from UP/Bihar/Bombay without local roots; elections postponed; Muslims weakened on both sides (45 million left in India). Partition constitutionalised enmity; defence budgets explode (Wavell’s ~100 crore vs post-partition ~250 crore for India, ~100+ for Pakistan). Geography against a consolidated Muslim state; East and West Pakistan already divergent; Islam alone has not held Muslim countries together after the first generations. Die cast: friendship between India and Pakistan the only sane course. History to judge whether partition was inevitable or avoidable — Azad’s vote is for the Cabinet Mission alternative.
