The History of British India, Vol. I (Mill / Wilson, 1840)

James Mill; fourth edition with notes and continuation by Horace Hayman Wilson, Boden Professor of Sanskrit, Oxford. Volume I of eight (half-title: Mill’s History of British India, by Wilson. In Eight Volumes. Vol. I.). This volume: British intercourse to the Act of 6 Anne / Godolphin award (1711), then Mill’s long Hindu survey.

Completeness

Complete as Volume I of the 1840 Wilson edition. Not the complete History.

  • Half-title: eight volumes; this file VOL. I.
  • Closer: END OF VOL. I. (E. Varty, 27 Camomile Street, Bishopsgate); UCLA Southern Regional Library Facility copy, 600 OCR leaves.
  • Book I (Company to 1711) and Book II (Hindus: chronology, caste, government, laws, taxes, religion, manners) are present.
  • Wilson’s continuation and later Books live in vols II–VIII, not in this ZIP.

Mill first published 1817 (three volumes). This is the annotated 1840 Madden set.

Summary

Wilson’s editor’s preface: Mill claimed patient research and independent judgment; Wilson supplies omissions from documents Mill lacked (Malcolm, Clive and Munro biographies, Wellesley and Wellington despatches) and corrects inaccuracies from Mill’s never having been in India and slight knowledge of Eastern languages. Mill had argued those were qualifications; Wilson: they will convince few. Local knowledge is needed to weigh Indian evidence; Mill often trusts the weakest authorities. European-language stock is not enough for Hindu and Mohammedan history; Vedas, Puranas, native chronicles, better Ferishta still wanting when Mill wrote.

Mill’s own Book I: commencement of British intercourse until the Company on a durable basis by 6 Anne. Five chapters: efforts to begin trade until the change from a regulated to a joint-stock company; joint-stock 1612 to the third joint-stock 1631–2; third stock 1632 to coalition with the Merchant Adventurers 1657; that coalition to the project of a rival company; rival company to the Award of Godolphin, 1711.

Book II, Of the Hindus: chronology and ancient history; classification and distribution of the people; form of government; laws; taxes; religion; manners. Wilson notes in the extract (end of vol.) on native character in Calcutta, against a wholly dark picture.

Contents

Printed contents: Preface; Glossary; Book I chs I–V (pp. 1–153); Book II chs I–VII (from p. 154; Manners from p. 437). Wilson’s notes are interleaved throughout (marked —W. against Mill’s —M.).

Preface of the Editor (Wilson)

Wilson grants Mill patient research and a school of independent judgment, then lists what the 1817 text could not use: unpublished papers, and later printed witnesses (Malcolm; lives of Clive and Munro; Wellesley and Wellington despatches). He rejects Mill’s claim that never having been in India, and slight knowledge of Eastern languages, were qualifications. Residence and dialects are needed to weigh Indian evidence; Mill, Wilson says, often trusts the weakest authorities or the least careful passages of the better ones. European-language stock is not enough for Hindu or Mohammedan history: Vedas, Purāṇas, native chronicles, and a trustworthy Ferishta were still wanting when Mill wrote.

Glossary

Listed in the contents at p. xxxvi of the printed volume (Indian and Company terms for the notes).

Book I — British intercourse to 6 Anne / Godolphin 1711

Chapter I — From the first efforts at an Indian trade to the change from a regulated to a joint-stock company

Mill opens with a full century of Portuguese monopoly after Vasco da Gama: exploration to Japan, island discoveries, conquests, and the flood of Eastern goods that had once been credited with raising Venice and delaying Constantinople’s fall. English attempts at a north-west passage (Frobisher’s ore that proved glittering sand; Davis’s straits) fail; a 1582 Cape voyage turns back from Brazil; a 1596 China voyage is wrecked. Drake’s circumnavigation and other successful runs then point the way. Adventurers form; Lancaster commands the first fleet; the charter of 31 December 1600 incorporates them as a body politic, in Mill’s view not remarkably unlike other trading charters of the age, yet the origin of a later anomalous power. Voyages remain separate adventures of subscribing groups under Company regulation until the Directors push a joint stock.

Chapter II — Joint-stock, 1612, to the third joint-stock, 1631–2

Until 1612 each voyage is the property of those who put money into that adventure. Directors argue that a joint stock concentrates management in the Governor and themselves; in 1612 they resolve that the trade shall be on a joint stock only. No fixed share-capital yet: members still subscribe unevenly, but into the Directors’ hands as one fund. £429,000 is raised and split into four successive yearly voyages. Mill follows the Company’s early eastern stations and contests from Bruce’s Annals, including the Dutch rivalry that later chapters resume.

Chapter III — Third joint-stock, 1632, to coalition with the Merchant Adventurers, 1657

A third subscription of £420,700 (1631–32) fits out seven ships; later seasons shrink to five then three, with cargoes unrecorded. Mill cannot tell whether earlier capital was repaid, though Directors are already without funds. Connexions on the Coromandel coast: Masulipatam factory restored and privileges from the King of Golconda; Mogul leave to trade at Piplee in Orissa; Bantam again a presidency over the eastern coast. Despair of beating the Dutch in the islands. Courten’s association, using Endymion Porter and the King’s favour after new arrangements with the Portuguese, appears as a licensed rival. The thread runs to the 1657 coalition with the Merchant Adventurers.

Chapter IV — Coalition with the Merchant Adventurers to the project of a rival company

After the Council of State decides for joint-stock trade, Company and Adventurers unite. A 1657–58 subscription reaches £786,000; whether the expected charter was actually received is left uncertain (Bruce found no copy). Adjustment with owners of the old funds; Dutch pressure on Portuguese Ceylon and a blockade of Goa, threatening Diu and Swally. The new stock is no more prosperous: feeble factories; two silent years of equipments; resolutions to drop out-stations and confine the peninsula to Surat, Fort St George, and their subordinates. Cromwell dies; Charles II; Polaroon delivered in “prepared desolation.” Petition for a new charter at the Restoration, granted with little organised opposition. The chapter closes as a new rival-company project comes into view.

Chapter V — Rival company to the Award of Godolphin, 1711

From the Restoration charter, Mill says, the monopoly is little disturbed except by crushable interlopers until 1682–83, when a subscription for a rival joint-stock is serious enough for King and Council. Bruce thinks the scare made the Company speak of equipments in “vague and hyperbolical” millions. Debts: opponents in 1676 put them at £600,000; Surat already £135,000 in 1674; Bengal agency authorised to borrow £200,000; dead stock at Bombay £300,000 — debts probably exceeding capital. Bantam lost in a local war (English accused the Dutch). The narrative then runs through the two companies’ parliamentary struggle to 6 Anne c. 17 and Godolphin’s award, 29 September 1708 (contents heading: 1711): London Company pays the United Company £96,615 4s. 9d. because Indian assets fall short of debts; English Company receives £66,005 4s. 2d. because its Indian effects exceed debts; a Bengal debt of Sir Edward Littleton remains on the English Company’s own account; British debts to be cleared by 1 March 1709, the London Company’s £399,795 9s. 1d. by three calls on proprietors. Mill ends Book I by announcing that English intercourse with India is now to become intimate and extensive — the subject of the next two books.

Book II — Of the Hindus

Chapter I — Chronology and ancient history of the Hindus

“Rude nations seem to derive a peculiar gratification from pretensions to a remote antiquity.” Mill classes Hindu claims with Irish, Tartar, English, Athenian, and Arcadian origin-legends, and with a Chaldee fragment of Babylonian ages. The chapter is a sceptical survey of Hindu chronology and ancient history as then available in European languages, with Wilson already interrupting on authorities.

Chapter II — Classification and distribution of the people

Transition from tribal independence to monarchy is slow: no common judge, no preconcerted punishment, then crowding, discord, and “superior spirits” who first found government on divine authority. Nowhere, Mill says, are laws more exclusively referred to the Divinity than in the “theocracy of Hindustan”: society, rights, even domestic custom by divine prescription. The four-varna scheme and the multiplication of castes follow. Wilson’s long note: caste among Buddhists is not original; multiplication of castes is the work of the people, not a code; the lowest native is a member of a class, unlike European paupers who are the true outcastes.

Chapter III — The form of government

After rank and occupation, the political establishment. “Among the Hindus, according to the Asiatic model, the government was monarchical, and, with the usual exception of religion and its ministers, absolute.” No idea of rule other than the will of a single person. Menu: a king formed of particles of the guardian deities; a child-king still a divinity in human shape; hatred of the king is death. Wilson had already flagged (at the laws chapter) that unbounded Brahman authority and unbounded royal power cannot both be simply true, and that in the original system the two were meant to check each other.

Chapter IV — The laws

Second determinant of political condition: how individual rights are expressed and secured. Materials are abundant; Mill offers only character and spirit, not an analysis of the whole code. Among rude peoples, maxims of justice are not kept distinct from other subjects; in Hindu law-books, jurisprudence occupies a moderate space beside religion, education, domestic custom, and private morality. Wilson vs Mill on English analogies (Hume, Blackstone, Paley, Bolingbroke on the Crown) and on the check between princes and Brahmans.

Chapter V — The taxes

Of government and law, nothing more decides happiness or misery than how the state’s pecuniary wants are met, and how far agents divide the annual produce of land and labour. Company servants’ financial perplexities of detail are set aside; Mill wants the outline in the ancient books. Menu: the king may take an eighth, sixth, or twelfth of grain (Culluca: by soil and labour); a sixth of the increase of trees, flesh, honey, ghee, perfumes, medicines, flowers, roots, fruit, leaves, pot-herbs, grass, leather, cane, earthen pots, stone-work; a fiftieth of the yearly addition to cattle, gems, gold, and silver. Other heads of levy follow in the ordinance list.

Chapter VI — Religion

Whether constitution and law or religion most sway Hindu lives is left open; priests, Mill says, obtained greater authority in India than anywhere, and multiplied the ideas on which their power stood. Laws, classification, and government are all “transacted by the Deity”; observances hedge every hour and function; meditation on divine attributes is the occupation that prepares the votary for participation in the divine nature. Only a general view is attempted: Brahman language on the gods is vague, fictions innumerable, ideas discrepant — “no coherent system of belief seems capable of being extracted.” Wilson’s notes on ritual and on Hindu “fate” as the consequence of past acts (not helpless destiny) sit against Mill’s picture.

Chapter VII — Manners

Manners = the peculiar modes of the ordinary business of life (eating, meeting, transacting, sport), which is everywhere the same business. So much of Hindu life is religious service that the religion chapter has already drawn the principal branch. Class distinctions: the lower orders “degraded below the brutes”; except Vaiśyas (agriculture and barter), productive classes are “vile and odious” by the standards of law and religion. Four āśramas for the honourable classes: student (more apprentice than literary pupil; even a Brahman’s child ranks as Śūdra before investiture), householder, forest penitent, sannyāsi. Wilson’s note D (end of the volume, after Calcutta residence): polished manners and independence among men of property; natives “estimable and amiable” who will requite kindness and justice from those who rule them.
